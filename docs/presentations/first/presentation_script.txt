======================================================================
SLIDE 1: TITLE (about 30 seconds)
======================================================================

Good afternoon. We are Riya, Sabarigirish and Breeti, and our project is
Aqua Edge.

Aqua Edge is an offline-first IoT system that detects three things in a
water network: unsafe water, pipeline leaks, and abnormal water use. It
does this in real time, and it keeps working even when the internet
connection is unreliable.


======================================================================
SLIDE 2: PROBLEM STATEMENT (about 1 minute)
======================================================================

Water networks face three problems.

First, unsafe water: contamination often goes unnoticed until people are
already affected.

Second, leaks: water escapes from pipes, sometimes in the main lines before
it ever reaches a meter.

Third, abnormal consumption: things like water running all night or sudden
spikes, which can mean a leak, a faulty meter or misuse.

Most smart water systems today send their sensor data to the cloud, and the
cloud makes the decisions. That works while the internet is up. But in rural
areas, underground sites or during outages, the link drops. When that
happens, unsafe water, leaks and abnormal use go undetected, exactly when
problems happen.

So our problem is: how do we keep detection working when connectivity fails?


======================================================================
SLIDE 3: LITERATURE REVIEW, PART 1 (about 1 minute 15 seconds)
======================================================================

We reviewed six papers. For each one, we looked at what it does and what we
can use from it.

Sharma and colleagues, 2026, put pH, TDS, temperature and turbidity sensors
on an ESP32 microcontroller, and a tiny neural network on the chip decides
whether the water is safe, with no cloud needed. We use this as our water
quality check. Its limit is that it only checks water quality, not leaks or
usage. And although it's designed to work offline, the authors never
actually tested it during an internet outage.

Alghamdi and colleagues, 2022, designed a leak detection system over LoRa,
a long-range, low-power radio. Each node has a water meter and a pressure
sensor, and when pressure drops below a set level, it's flagged as a leak.
We take this pressure-drop rule for detecting leaks. They tested it in a
network simulator, and they measured message delivery, not how accurately
leaks were found.

Babazadeh, 2019, showed that a small node can spot unusual readings by
itself and send only those over LoRa, which saves data and battery. We
borrow this idea of deciding on the node. Its examples were pressure events,
not everyday water usage.


======================================================================
SLIDE 4: LITERATURE REVIEW, PART 2 (about 1 minute 15 seconds)
======================================================================

Mariadhason and colleagues, 2026, built a water quality system that
timestamps readings and stores them on an SD card when the connection fails,
then sends them once it's back. We use this store-and-forward idea so no
data is lost offline. But it only handles water quality, and it doesn't test
longer and longer outages.
(Say "Mariadhason and colleagues", not "HydroSense", so nobody confuses
their system name with Aqua Edge.)

Pagano and colleagues, 2024, created SWI-FEED, a testing framework that
combines EPANET, which simulates water flowing in pipes, with NS-3, which
simulates the radio network. We will use the same kind of setup to test our
system. It doesn't simulate network outages, so we will add those ourselves.

Finally, Ali and colleagues, 2022, is the closest to our project. One
prototype handles water quality, water usage and leaks. It detects leaks by
checking whether the water going in matches the water coming out. We use
this flow-balance check, and this system is our cloud-based baseline to
compare against, because all its decisions are made in the cloud.

So, putting it together: each piece exists separately, and the one system
that combines them depends on the cloud. That leads us to our research gap.


======================================================================
SLIDE 5: RESEARCH GAP (about 45 seconds)
======================================================================

From these papers, we found three things.

One: each task has already been done at the edge, meaning on the device
itself, but separately. There's water quality on one device, leak detection
on another, and anomaly detection on another.

Two: the one system we found that combines all three still makes its
decisions in the cloud.

Three, and this is our gap: in the papers we reviewed, nobody measures how
detection and alerts hold up when the internet is unavailable, compared with
a cloud-based system.

So the question isn't just "can we combine them?" It's "does doing it on the
device actually help when the connection fails?"


======================================================================
SLIDE 6: RESEARCH QUESTION AND HYPOTHESIS (about 1 minute)
======================================================================

That gives us our research question.

How does an offline-first edge system that detects water-quality problems,
leaks and abnormal consumption compare with a cloud-dependent system, in
detection accuracy, alert latency and energy use, when the internet is
unavailable?

In simple terms, we're comparing two designs. In one, each device makes its
own decisions. In the other, everything waits for the cloud. And we compare
them on three things:
- Accuracy: does it catch the real problems?
- Alert latency: how quickly does the alert arrive?
- Energy: how much battery does it use?

Our hypothesis: the offline-first system will stay about as accurate as the
cloud system, but give faster alerts and keep working during internet
outages, because it doesn't depend on the connection.

We'll test this in simulation by cutting the network for different lengths
of time and measuring what each system still manages to do.

Q&A PREPARATION
======================================================================

Q: How is your hypothesis testable?
A: If the edge system loses a lot of accuracy, or doesn't deliver alerts
   faster during outages, our hypothesis is wrong.

Q: What does "comparable accuracy" mean?
A: We'll set an acceptable accuracy difference once our evaluation plan is
   approved.

Q: Your hypothesis doesn't mention energy. Why is it in the question?
A: We'll measure energy to see the cost of running detection on the device.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Why does the combined paper (Ali et al., 2022) do detection in the cloud?
A: Their leak method compares flow from several nodes at the same moment
   (water in vs water out), so they gathered everything in one central
   place, a cloud database (Firebase), which also gave users and admins a
   dashboard. It was a small home prototype on reliable Wi-Fi, so outages
   weren't their focus. Their nodes store data offline, but the leak
   decision waits for the cloud. We move that decision onto the devices and
   the gateway, so it keeps working when the link is down.

Q: So how do you compare flows from several nodes without the cloud?
A: The gateway does it. It's local and receives data from all the nodes over
   LoRa, with no internet needed.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: What is the challenge in combining all three? What's the actual gap?
A (20-second version):
   Combining them isn't just stacking three detectors. They compete for one
   small device's memory and battery, they work on different time scales,
   the same signal like a pressure drop can mean a leak or a supply problem,
   and some checks need data from other nodes, which normally comes from the
   cloud. Nobody has measured how a combined system handles these trade-offs
   when offline. That's our gap.

The five challenges in detail:
1. Limited device resources: one ESP32 has little memory, a slow processor
   and a small battery, and now runs three detectors plus storage and radio.
   Each paper ran only one task.
2. Different time scales: leaks need checks every few seconds, water quality
   changes over minutes, abnormal use needs days of history. Faster readings
   catch leaks sooner but use more battery.
3. The same signal can mean different things: a pressure drop could be a
   leak, heavy normal use, or the whole supply weakening. A usage spike
   could be a leak or someone filling a tank. Detectors running together
   can contradict each other or raise false alarms.
4. Some checks need other nodes: the flow-balance check and the
   supply-event check need data from several nodes. Offline, that job moves
   to a local gateway.
5. Surviving outages: during a long outage the SD card fills up and the
   radio can only send small amounts, so the system must decide what to
   keep, what to send first (alerts before routine data), and keep
   timestamps correct.

The actual gap in one sentence:
   Nobody has measured whether one low-cost device can run all three
   detections on its own, and how well it still performs as internet
   outages get longer, compared with a cloud-based system.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Does each node have an ESP32?
A: Yes. Each node has its own ESP32, so every node makes its own decisions
   and keeps working offline. One node = ESP32 + sensors (pH, TDS,
   turbidity, temperature, flow, pressure) + LoRa radio module + SD card +
   battery. The ESP32 has no LoRa built in, so we add a LoRa module (or use
   a combined LoRa32 board). The gateway is a separate device.
   We're doing simulation first, so most nodes will be virtual; hardware,
   if built, would be one or two real nodes.

Q: Why an ESP32?
A: It's cheap, low-power, has enough memory to run the small water-quality
   model, and the paper we build on already showed the model runs on it.

Q: Why LoRa?
A: Long range and low power for small sensor messages, and it works without
   internet, which fits an offline-first water network.

Q: Is Alghamdi et al. a real paper if it's "only a simulation"?
A: Yes, it's published in Sensors (2022). "Simulation" describes how they
   tested it: a virtual housing complex in the FLoRa simulator on OMNeT++,
   with 72 to 1,800 nodes, instead of real hardware.
